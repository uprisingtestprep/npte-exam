Practice Test 1 Answer Key

  1. The correct answer is B (The supraspinatus tendon and subacromial bursa beneath the coracoacromial arch): the Neer test forward flexes the internally rotated humerus so the greater tuberosity and its attached supraspinatus tendon slide beneath the anterior third of the acromion and coracoacromial ligament. The subacromial bursa is compressed simultaneously, reproducing pain in classic subacromial impingement. Internal rotation is deliberately added to further narrow the subacromial space during the maneuver.
  2. The correct answer is B (Subacromial impingement of the rotator cuff tendons beneath the acromion): the Hawkins-Kennedy test uses ninety degrees of flexion followed by forced internal rotation to drive the greater tuberosity and rotator cuff tendons against the coracoacromial ligament. This position narrows the subacromial space even more than the Neer position for some patients. A positive test corroborates a clinical impression of impingement rather than instability or joint degeneration.
  3. The correct answer is B (A positive drop arm sign with inability to lower the arm smoothly): a full thickness supraspinatus tear often produces frank weakness that manifests as the arm dropping suddenly or the patient being unable to control lowering from an abducted position. Tendinopathy without a complete tear typically preserves the ability to control the descent, even if painful. The drop arm sign therefore helps differentiate a structural tear from irritation alone.
  4. The correct answer is C (The extensor carpi radialis brevis tendon at its lateral epicondyle origin): lateral epicondylalgia, commonly called tennis elbow, most consistently involves degenerative changes at the origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis. Cozen's test loads this tendon by resisting wrist extension with the elbow extended, reproducing the patient's characteristic lateral elbow pain. The other listed tendons originate or insert at different regions unrelated to this presentation.
  5. The correct answer is C (Cervical radiculopathy from foraminal narrowing compressing an exiting nerve root): Spurling's test combines extension, lateral flexion, and rotation toward the symptomatic side with axial compression to further narrow an already stenotic intervertebral foramen. This reproduces radicular symptoms when a nerve root is being compressed. The maneuver is specifically designed to provoke foraminal encroachment rather than vascular or temporomandibular structures.
  6. The correct answer is B (Median nerve compression within the carpal tunnel at the wrist): numbness in the thumb, index, and middle fingers along with nocturnal symptoms and positive Tinel and Phalen findings is the classic presentation of carpal tunnel syndrome. The median nerve travels through the confined carpal tunnel at the volar wrist, making it susceptible to compression from swelling or repetitive strain. Ulnar and radial nerve distributions would produce a different sensory pattern.
  7. The correct answer is D (Abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis at the first compartment): Finkelstein's test stretches the tendons of the first dorsal compartment by combining thumb flexion into the palm with ulnar deviation of the wrist. Pain reproduced along the radial styloid confirms de Quervain's tenosynovitis affecting these two tendons. The other tendon pairs listed occupy different compartments not stressed by this maneuver.
  8. The correct answer is D (The bicipital groove along the anterior aspect of the proximal humerus): Speed's test loads the long head of biceps tendon by resisting shoulder flexion with the elbow extended and forearm supinated, which increases tension through the tendon as it courses within the bicipital groove. Reproduction of pain at this specific location supports biceps tendinopathy. The other structures listed are not directly loaded by this particular resisted motion.
  9. The correct answer is B (Biceps tendon instability or tendinopathy within the bicipital groove): Yergason's test resists forearm supination with the elbow flexed and stabilized, which specifically loads the long head of biceps tendon as it sits within the bicipital groove. Reproduction of pain at this groove supports biceps tendon pathology rather than acromioclavicular or glenohumeral joint disease. The stabilized elbow position isolates the biceps tendon from other shoulder structures.
  10. The correct answer is B (The Watson scaphoid shift test for scapholunate instability assessment): the Watson test applies dorsal pressure over the scaphoid tubercle while moving the wrist from ulnar to radial deviation, which normally causes the scaphoid to flex palmarly. A painful clunk indicates the scaphoid subluxes dorsally due to scapholunate ligament insufficiency. This test is specifically designed to detect that instability rather than tendon or artery pathology.
  11. The correct answer is C (The anterior cruciate ligament stabilizing anterior tibial translation): the Lachman test is the most sensitive clinical test for anterior cruciate ligament integrity, performed at thirty degrees of knee flexion where the hamstrings provide less restraining influence. Excessive anterior tibial translation with a soft, mushy end feel indicates a torn anterior cruciate ligament. A noncontact injury with an audible pop is a classic mechanism for this specific ligament tear.
  12. The correct answer is B (Posterior sag sign, indicating pre-existing posterior cruciate ligament laxity): when the tibia rests posteriorly relative to the femur before any drawer force is applied, this indicates the posterior cruciate ligament is already lax, allowing gravity to pull the tibia backward. Recognizing this sag prevents the examiner from mistaking the starting position for a normal one and misinterpreting subsequent drawer testing. This sign is a hallmark finding of posterior cruciate ligament insufficiency.
  13. The correct answer is C (A tear of the medial meniscus reproduced by this compression maneuver): McMurray's test combines knee extension from flexion with external tibial rotation and a valgus force to compress and shift the medial meniscus, and a palpable or audible click with pain during this maneuver indicates a medial meniscal tear. Medial joint line pain and mechanical locking further support this diagnosis. Internal rotation combined with varus stress would instead test the lateral meniscus.
  14. The correct answer is B (Meniscal tear identified through weight-bearing rotational loading of the knee): the Thessaly test applies rotational load to the knee while weight bearing, which compresses and shears the menisci in a manner similar to functional activities. Joint line discomfort or a sense of locking during this rotation is considered a positive finding for meniscal pathology. This weight bearing approach differs from non-weight bearing tests like McMurray's or Apley's.
  15. The correct answer is A (The tensor fasciae latae and iliotibial band complex laterally): Ober's test assesses the length of the tensor fasciae latae and its iliotibial band by abducting and extending the hip then allowing gravity to adduct the leg. Failure of the leg to drop past neutral indicates tightness in this lateral hip and thigh complex rather than the iliopsoas, piriformis, or adductors, which are tested through different positions.
  16. The correct answer is D (The sacroiliac joint stressed by the combined hip and pelvic positioning): the FABER position combines flexion, abduction, and external rotation of the hip in a way that transmits stress through the pelvis to the sacroiliac joint, and posterior sacral pain during this maneuver suggests sacroiliac joint involvement. Anterior groin pain instead would more strongly implicate the hip joint itself. This distinction helps the examiner localize the true source of symptoms.
  17. The correct answer is C (The iliopsoas hip flexor group on the side being tested): during Thomas's test, pulling the opposite knee to the chest flattens the lumbar spine and stabilizes the pelvis, and if the tested hip cannot fully extend so the thigh remains elevated off the table, this indicates tightness of the iliopsoas hip flexor group on that side. Hamstring or gluteal tightness would not produce this specific compensatory thigh elevation pattern.
  18. The femoral nerve is instead tested with the prone knee bend maneuver.
  19. The correct answer is A (The meniscal cartilage compressed between the tibia and femur): Apley's compression test applies downward force through the tibia while rotating it, which compresses the menisci between the femoral condyles and tibial plateau, and pain during compression with rotation supports a meniscal tear. Apley's distraction component, by contrast, would be used to assess ligamentous rather than meniscal structures. Prone positioning stabilizes the femur during this maneuver.
  20. Trochanteric bursitis and piriformis syndrome typically present with lateral or posterior symptoms rather than this specific anterior clicking pattern reproduced by hip flexion and rotation.
  21. Grade one and two oscillations are used earlier in range primarily for pain modulation rather than stretching a firm end feel.
  22.   23.   24. For the glenohumeral joint, external rotation is most limited, followed by abduction, then internal rotation, and this proportional loss with a firm capsular end feel distinguishes it from a noncapsular or neurological pattern of restriction.
  25. During flexion the humeral head rolls anteriorly on the fixed glenoid, and per the convex-concave rule, convex-on-concave motion produces roll and glide in opposite directions, so the corrective accessory glide is applied posteriorly, opposite the roll, to maintain joint congruency and restore the motion.
  26. Both can be used at various joints, and end feel assessment remains essential before either technique.
  27.   28.   29. This targeted mobilization directly addresses the specific motion limited after immobilization for a Colles fracture.
  30.   31.   32.
